## The problem it solves

Six months of revenue is easy to look at. Six months of five metrics by six customers, two channels and five products is not: some numbers always wobble, a seasonal peak is not news, and "revenue fell" is useless until you know *where* it fell. A model asked to eyeball the table will find patterns in noise and do the subtraction in its head.

So detection is a fixed library of tests, each one SQL plus a small function, and every number it produces is a [fact](../../glossary/#fact) with an ID.

**Compare.** March revenue was <Fact id={ch.before} />, April's <Fact id={ch.after} />. The change, <Fact id={ch.delta} />, and the percentage, <Fact id={ch.pct} />, are *function facts*: `delta` and `pct_change` applied to the two SQL facts, so each one points at its inputs.

**Is it bigger than the noise?** Every series wobbles. The engine measures how much revenue usually moves and asks whether April is outside that. Two tests agree here:

- a [robust z-score](../../glossary/#robust-z-score) of <Fact id={a.noise.z} />, against a threshold of ±{a.noise.threshold}: far outside the usual wobble;
- a [noise band](../../glossary/#noise-band) of ±{a.noise.band.toFixed(1)}, against a level shift of <Fact id={a.noise.shift} /> after March (template <code>{a.noise.template}</code>). The band is not a fact but a parameter of the test, stored in the insight's details: 2 × 1.4826 × the median absolute deviation of the series' other month-to-month changes, so the change being tested can't widen its own band (`noiseBand` in [`baselines.ts`](https://github.com/gfirik/agentic-analytics/blob/main/packages/analysis/src/baselines.ts)).

March's own rise from February was small enough to stay inside the band, so it is not reported at all. On this run {a.templates.length} candidate insights came out of {templates.length} templates ({templates.join(", ")}); ranking keeps the ones that matter.

## The ideas behind it

### Facts, not answers

Each number is stored with its computation: the SQL and parameters, or the function and the IDs of its inputs. That is what lets the [verifier](../verifier/) recompute it later, and you open it from its chip.

### Robust statistics

An ordinary z-score uses the mean and standard deviation, which one outlier can drag around. A *robust* z-score uses the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) instead, so a single strange month cannot hide another. A value beyond about 3.5 robust z is a common rule of thumb for "unusual".

### Exact decompositions

Contribution, price-volume-mix and rate-versus-mix are identities: the parts always add up to the whole, and a test checks it on random data. There is also a Simpson's-paradox guard: when a rate moves one way in every group but the other way in total, the engine reports the mix shift instead of a misleading headline.

### Seasonality, on real data

A December peak in retail is not news. The engine compares a month with the same month in earlier years when it has them, and doesn't report a move that happens every year. The coffee example has only six months, so this is shown on the public datasets of the [real-data evals](../../../quality/evals/) (<code>{a.realFile}</code>):

## Try it

The first tutorial's job prints these findings; `aa explain` opens any fact:

```bash title="Terminal"
AA_DEMO_MODEL=scripted bun run aa analyze examples/coffee-roaster --yes --embedder hash --today 2026-07-01 --currency USD
bun run aa explain <job-id> N30
```
